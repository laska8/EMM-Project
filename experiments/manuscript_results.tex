\paragraph{M. Activity-based reference subgroup discovery}
The binary search returned 29 valid subgroups: nine with one condition and twenty with two. All 29 reciprocity coefficients were negative, ranging from -0.522 to -0.177, against a global reference of -0.2413. The highest-quality subgroup was Sent $>$ 3 and Received $>$ 3 (n = 5,056,348; return rate 75.78\%; beta = -0.522; 95\% CI [-0.537, -0.507]). Its AME was -7.55 percentage points. The quality score was sqrt(n) times the absolute coefficient difference from the reference. 28/29 subgroup intervals excluded the reference point, which is reported as a descriptive diagnostic rather than a formal test of the subgroup-reference difference. Subgroup definitions used activity variables also included as controls.

\paragraph{A1. Reciprocity operationalization}
Binary and proportional reciprocity retained nine of the ten highest-quality subgroup descriptions (Jaccard overlap 9/11 = 0.818). All nine shared top-ten subgroups had negative coefficients and more negative coefficients than their own measure-specific references. Across all 29 shared groups, coefficient signs agreed in 29/29 cases, while the direction of the subgroup-reference difference agreed in 21/29. Thus the main selected contexts and association signs were broadly stable, but ranking and some reference-relative conclusions depended on the definition of reciprocity. The weighted variant was not applicable: an exhaustive audit of the supplied raw networks found no repeated targets in any outgoing user-month list, so repeated-reply intensity was unavailable. Coefficients under the two measures refer to different contrasts and should not be interpreted as directly comparable probability changes.

\paragraph{A2. Dependence between observations}
User clustering increased the reference standard error by 13.1\% and top-ten subgroup standard errors by 2.1\%-10.8\% (median 5.9\%). All ten subgroup confidence intervals remained below zero under both approaches. Coefficients and the quality ranking were unchanged. M already used user-clustered inference; A2 supplies its explicit comparison with ordinary model-based uncertainty. A subgroup confidence interval excluding the global reference coefficient is a descriptive diagnostic, not a formal test of the difference between two estimated coefficients. The reference includes the subgroup, so a formal test must account for their joint uncertainty. Intervals are pointwise and have no multiple-testing or post-selection adjustment.

\paragraph{A3. Probability-scale comparison}
The reference AME was -4.44 percentage points. AMEs for the fixed top ten ranged from -9.01 to -5.59 percentage points. All ten subgroup-reference differences had the same direction on the coefficient and probability scales. However, the Spearman correlation between rankings by absolute coefficient difference and absolute AME difference was only 0.370. Directional conclusions were consistent, while the ranking of substantive effect magnitudes depended on the scale. The most negative AME was -9.01 percentage points for Sent$>$1 and Received$<$=3, rather than for the subgroup with the most negative coefficient. AMEs were evaluated over each model's own observations, and no AME confidence intervals were estimated.

\paragraph{Scope and interpretation}
These completed analyses use months 1-10, activity selectors and the two contemporaneous activity controls, with month and subreddit fixed effects. They do not include a community-context selector search or lagged controls. Results are descriptive rather than causal; pointwise intervals have no post-selection adjustment.
